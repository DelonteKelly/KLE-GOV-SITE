Equity Follows the Law

Primary Collection: Equity & RemediesRelated: Fiduciary Foundations, Trust Administration, Governance Systems
I. Executive Summary

Equity is not a shortcut around law. Equity operates where legal remedies are inadequate, where conscience requires intervention, and where fiduciary or trust obligations require enforcement. Equity follows the law by supplementing legal rights and remedies where justice requires, but it does not disregard valid law, procedure, jurisdiction, or clean hands.

Equity respects legal rights while supplying remedies such as injunction, specific performance, rescission, reformation, constructive trust, equitable lien, accounting, and surcharge. A party seeking equity must come with clean hands, act with diligence, and respect legal procedures. Equity aids the vigilant, not those who sleep on their rights.

Why It Matters: Equity is a discipline of higher accountability, not escape from legal obligations. Fiduciaries and governance participants who invoke equity must demonstrate clean hands, good faith, record integrity, and proper capacity.
II. Core Principle

Equity follows the law by supplementing legal rights and remedies where justice requires, but it does not disregard valid law, procedure, jurisdiction, or clean hands.

III. Governance Rule

No party may invoke equity while ignoring procedure, jurisdiction, record integrity, clean hands, good faith, or valid legal obligations. Equity requires disciplined record, proper capacity, and accountable conduct.

IV. Doctrinal Explanation
  • Equity Follows the Law: Equity does not override or nullify valid legal rules. It fills gaps where legal remedies are inadequate and enforces obligations that law recognizes but cannot remedy fully.
  • Equity Acts In Personam: Equity operates on the person, compelling specific action or prohibiting conduct through injunction or decree. It is not a general override of legal title but a remedy directed at the conscience of the party.
  • Equity Requires Clean Hands: A party seeking equitable relief must come with clean hands—meaning they must not have engaged in inequitable conduct relating to the same subject matter. Unclean hands bars equitable relief regardless of the merits of the underlying claim.
  • Equity Aids the Vigilant: Laches bars stale claims. A party who unreasonably delays seeking equitable relief may be denied remedy even if the underlying claim has merit.
  • Equity Prevents Unjust Enrichment: Where a party receives or retains a benefit that in equity and good conscience belongs to another, equity may impose a constructive trust or equitable lien to prevent unjust enrichment.
  • Equity Enforces Fiduciary Obligations: Equity has primary jurisdiction over trusts and fiduciary relationships, supplying remedies including accounting, surcharge, removal, and disgorgement of profits.
  • Equity Supplies Remedies When Legal Relief Is Inadequate: Where money damages are insufficient to remedy harm, equity may provide injunctive relief, specific performance, rescission, or reformation.
  • Equity Does Not Create Immunity from Jurisdiction: Equity operates within established legal jurisdiction. It does not excuse a party from appearing, responding, or complying with procedural rules.
  • Equity Does Not Excuse Defective Procedure: Procedural defects, lack of standing, failure to state a claim, or improper service are not cured merely by invoking equity.
  • Equity Depends on Conscience, Good Faith, and Record Integrity: Equitable relief is discretionary, requiring the court to assess the conduct of all parties, the adequacy of records, and the presence of good faith.
V. Recognized Authorities
  • Pomeroy, Equity Jurisprudence § 1 – Equity is a system of justice administered in cases where legal remedies are inadequate, supplementing but not contradicting law.
  • Story, Commentaries on Equity Jurisprudence § 1 – Equity follows the law, respecting legal rights while supplying remedies where justice requires intervention.
  • Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 1 – A person who is unjustly enriched at the expense of another is subject to liability in restitution.
  • Restatement (Third) of Trusts § 78 – The duty of loyalty is enforceable in equity, with remedies including disgorgement and constructive trust.
  • Uniform Trust Code § 802 – Duty of loyalty, enforceable through equitable remedies including removal, surcharge, and constructive trust.
  • Uniform Trust Code § 1001 – Remedies for breach of trust include surcharge, removal, accounting, injunction, constructive trust, and equitable lien.
  • Grupo Mexicano de Desarrollo, S.A. v. Alliance Bond Fund, Inc., 527 U.S. 308 (1999) – Equity follows the law; equitable remedies are not unlimited and must respect legal principles and congressional intent.
  • Hecht Co. v. Bowles, 321 U.S. 321 (1944) – Equitable relief is discretionary, not mandatory, and courts consider the conduct of all parties.
  • Weinberger v. Romero-Barcelo, 456 U.S. 305 (1982) – A court must balance the equities before granting injunctive relief; equity is not automatic.

These authorities reflect broadly recognized equitable principles. Specific application depends on jurisdiction, facts, procedural posture, governing instruments, and competent professional review.

VI. Operational Application
  • Fiduciary Breach: Equity provides remedies for breach of fiduciary duty, including accounting, surcharge, constructive trust, equitable lien, and removal.
  • Trust Administration: Equity enforces trust terms, compels accountings, and protects beneficiary interests through equitable remedies.
  • Constructive Trust: Where a party holds property that in equity belongs to another, equity may impose a constructive trust to restore the property to its rightful owner.
  • Equitable Lien: Equity may impose a lien on property to secure restitution when a party has been unjustly enriched.
  • Injunction: A court may enjoin threatened or continuing wrongful conduct where legal remedies are inadequate.
  • Rescission: A contract may be rescinded for fraud, mistake, or undue influence, restoring parties to their pre-contract positions.
  • Reformation: A written instrument may be reformed to reflect the parties' actual agreement where mistake or fraud is proven.
  • Accounting: A fiduciary may be compelled to render a full accounting of all receipts, disbursements, and transactions.
  • Institutional Review: Equity requires that institutional records be maintained with integrity and that participants act in good faith.
VII. Capacity Distinction

Private Individual Capacity: A person seeking equitable relief in private capacity must demonstrate clean hands, standing, and inadequacy of legal remedy.

Representative / Fiduciary Capacity: A fiduciary seeking equitable relief must act within the scope of fiduciary authority and must not breach duties of loyalty or care.

Trustee Capacity: A trustee may seek equitable guidance, instructions, or relief, but must do so in trustee capacity, not individually, and must provide full disclosure.

Institutional / Office Capacity: An organization may seek equitable relief, but officers acting for the organization must have proper authority and clean hands.

Equitable relief depends on proper capacity and clean hands. A party acting in the wrong capacity lacks standing to seek equitable remedies.

VIII. Recordkeeping Requirements
  • Governing instrument (trust, contract, resolution) creating the relationship.
  • Duty record identifying fiduciary or contractual obligations.
  • Breach record documenting the alleged violation of duty.
  • Accounting record tracking all relevant receipts, disbursements, and transactions.
  • Notice record proving communication with adverse parties and beneficiaries.
  • Evidence of inadequacy of legal remedy for equitable relief sought.
  • Clean hands record demonstrating equitable conduct by the party seeking relief.
  • Good faith communications with affected parties.
  • Transaction history documenting the subject matter of the dispute.
  • Beneficiary communications, consents, and objections.
  • Remedy request record explaining the equitable relief sought and basis.
  • Review memorandum analyzing the application of equitable principles.
  • Signature capacity on all documents submitted to court or reviewers.

Core rule: Equity follows the law and depends on record integrity. Without records, clean hands cannot be proven, and equitable relief may be denied.

IX. Common Errors
  • Treating equity as law avoidance. Equity is not a way to disregard valid law, procedure, or jurisdiction.
  • Invoking equity without clean hands. Unclean conduct bars equitable relief regardless of the merits of the claim.
  • Ignoring legal procedure. Procedural defects are not cured by invoking equity.
  • Claiming remedies without evidence. Equitable relief requires proof, not mere invocation of equitable language.
  • Failing to show inadequate legal remedy. Equity generally does not apply where money damages would suffice.
  • Confusing equitable title with unrestricted control. Equitable title is a beneficial interest, not a license to ignore legal title or fiduciary duties.
  • Using equity language without fiduciary basis. Equitable remedies require a recognized equitable relationship, such as trust, fiduciary duty, or unjust enrichment.
  • Failing to preserve accounting records. Without accounting, equitable remedies such as surcharge and constructive trust cannot be properly determined.
  • Seeking remedy without capacity. A party without standing or proper capacity cannot obtain equitable relief.
  • Relying on slogans instead of doctrine. "Equity follows the law" is a principle with specific requirements, not a rhetorical device.
X. Institutional Rationale

KLI teaches equity as a discipline of higher accountability, not escape. Equity is not a shortcut around legal obligations, procedure, or jurisdiction. It is a system of remedies that enforces fiduciary duties, prevents unjust enrichment, and protects beneficial interests where legal remedies are inadequate. The Institute preserves equitable doctrine to ensure that governance participants understand the requirements of clean hands, good faith, record integrity, and procedural discipline that equity demands.
